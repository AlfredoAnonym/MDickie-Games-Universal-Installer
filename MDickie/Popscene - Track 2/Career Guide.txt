* This manual deals with the theory behind the Career mode. For more information about the functionality of the game, please read "Instruction Manual.txt"...

Popscene is an incredibly detailed simulation of the music industry. Although the simple presentation makes it easy to enjoy, you may find that you get more out of the game by understanding how the world works. The following guide explains how real-life principles are manifested in the game...

--------------------------------------------------- THE LABELS ---------------------------------------------------

The world consists of 6 unique record labels:

- EPIC TALENT is the home of the most legendary artists in the world. These veterans have proved themselves as the Kings and Queens of their profession. Although they could be considered "past their best", their star power is often worth the astronomical asking price. Artists signed to this label enjoy the most generous advances in the business...

- SUPER SOUNDS is the home of the Princes and Princesses of pop. As artists, they still have a lot to prove - but their commerical appeal cannot be faulted. The world's most exciting and energetic performers can often be found here. Artists signed to this label enjoy the most generous music royalties in the business...

- ROCK-OUT RECORDS is the home of indie/rock bands that take their music more seriously. These artists tend to shun the limelight in an effort to hone their talent and writing skills. However, the mob-mentality of most bands makes them hard to separate. Artists signed to this label enjoy the most generous ticket royalties in the business...

- URBAN RECORDS is the home of hip hop; boasting some of the most controversial artists in the business. Their troublesome attitudes and dubious reputations make them difficult to deal with, but the pure talent beneath the surface is hard to ignore. Artists signed to this label enjoy the most generous endorsement royalties in the business...

- THE UNDERGROUND is where all aspiring young artists (or washed up old ones) strive to make their name. True talent is hard to find here - and even harder to market thanks to the lack of star power - but they're willing and eager to be transformed. Artists operating at this level are independently produced, which often means they take a large cut of a minimal income...

- LEGACY RECORDS is the resting place of all deceased artists. Their contribution to the industry might have reached its conclusion, but they can still enjoy chart success from beyond the grave. This is the one label that you shouldn't aspire to work for!

--------------------------------------------------- GETTING A DEAL ---------------------------------------------------

Naturally, any career starts on The Underground. Although not technically a record label, this independent circuit does have a boss who is there to guide your efforts. His patience is limited though. There's only so long he's willing to invest time in you before it becomes apparent that your career isn't going anywhere. Every week you spend there, then, is a race against the clock to get a record deal. Life without the backing of a major label is pretty miserable anyway, so it's a good job that you have the incentive!

Deals are inextricably linked to how popular and valuable your act is. Until you start making headway, there's very little chance that you will ever be approached. Even when you are getting somewhere, a lot of patience (and a little luck) is required to find your opportunity. When it finally happens, you will find yourself sitting opposite the boss of a label talking about a contract. This consists of an advance, royalties for music sales, ticket sales, and endorsements, and the length of time that this agreement applies. A modest contract will be suggested, but you then have the power to make changes and submit it back. Doing so is vital to get what you deserve, but testing the patience of the boss is extremely risky. There's only so many times you can push your luck before the entire meeting falls through...

Many artists are relieved to get a deal of any kind and often overlook the small print. Doing so can be the difference between success and failure. You will almost certainly be taken advantage of in the early days, so it's important to pay attention to how long you tie yourself down for. The contract length is set in stone as far as you're concerned, because it can only be terminated by the label. Although it is possible to be bought out of your contract by a rival company, such gestures are extremely rare. For this reason, you should think carefully before committing to any deal. Confident artists have been known to turn down early offers, safe in the knowledge that a better one lies down the road...

--------------------------------------------------- SIGNING TALENT ---------------------------------------------------

You see both sides of contract negotiations, because you must sign artists to your regime as surely as labels sign you to theirs. You can approach any artist in the world to join your team, whereon the same battle of wills takes place (only this time you're trying to negotiate the most reasonable deal rather than the most extravagant!). You must propose the initial contract and the artist will then declare any concerns he/she has. These will mainly concern the signing-on fee. Royalties are a mere luxury compared to the pay-off that they can expect for simply committing to the contract. As you'd expect, then, the fee is inextricably linked to how long you want them for. Artists want the most money for the least amount of work, whereas you want the exact opposite - so let battle commence!

When flustered by these demands, most managers resort to lowering the contract length - but it's important to consider your options. Although it's tempting to keep the contracts short and sweet, you will often find that it's not good value for money. This is especially true of growing artists, who can expect a pay rise at every opportunity. A long contract is worth every penny if it keeps an important artist tied down to a reasonable deal...

Sometimes the signing-on fee is the least of your concerns though. Artists can have altogether more personal reservations that need to be dealt with. Whether it's your status or your choice of friends, there are countless details that an artist can take exception to. Although these situations can be awkward, they're not the end of the world. Almost all of them can be alleviated with money! A more generous pay-off, or combination of royalties, can persuade most artists to sign anything. They've even been known to trade in their precious royalties for a juicier pay-off...

The label bosses are a little harder to buy though, and won't allow their artists to work for you as long as they're under contract. Your own boss has no such concerns though, as label-mates are free to collaborate with each other at will. That is yet another thing to consider when choosing where to work...

-------------------------------------------- WHAT MAKES A GOOD ARTIST? -----------------------------------------

An artist's value is determined by a combination of 8 unique qualities:

- POPULARITY indicates an artist's drawing power. This is arguably the most important quality, because even the most talented of artists can't sell CD's or tickets until they make their name. It's also the most precious quality, because true popularity is hard to obtain - and even harder to keep! It's attained over time with consistent success in the charts and on the road...

- REPUTATION is the level of respect that an artist commands from the outside world. Those with solid reputations tend to attract a loyal following that can be relied upon no matter how small it is. Like popularity, reputation is attained over time with success - but it can be spent all too easily by making bad decisions...  

- ATTITUDE indicates an artist's passion for the job - and their ability to get it done. Those with bad attitudes are usually lazy, demanding, and unco-operative. Even the most talented of artists can do more harm than good if their attitude isn't in check. You can cultivate a good attitude (or prevent a bad one) by standing your ground in meetings instead of giving in to demands...

- HAPPINESS is simply how happy an artist is with their career. Naturally, it's closely linked to how much success the artist is experiencing - but personal issues can have an even bigger impact. A lack of respect or peace is the workplace can put a frown on anybody's face. Unhappy artists are more likely to complain, and are less likely to renew their contract with you...

- PRODUCTION is an artist's ability to produce entertaining songs in the studio. For more information about this, please read "What Makes A Good Song?"...

- WRITING is an artist's ability to produce artistic songs in the studio. For more information about this, please read "What Makes A Good Song?"...

- PERFORMANCE is an artist's ability to deliver entertaining songs at a live show.  For more information about this, please read "What Makes A Good Performance?"...

- TALENT is an artist's ability to deliver artistic songs at a live show.  For more information about this, please read "What Makes A Good Performance?"...

-------------------------------------------- WHAT MAKES A GOOD BAND? --------------------------------------------

All of the above qualities can be compromised when thrown into a team environment. For a start, the first 4 status qualities become the average of each contributing artist. A guy with a bad reputation and attitude can taint what would have otherwise been a smooth operation. Inversely, a faultering team can be rejuvenated by hiring a solid role model... 

This is even true of popularity. However, that can be sharpened with a sensible formation. Unlike the other qualities, popularity is governed strictly by those that are on-stage. By keeping unpopular artists behind the scenes and reserving the limelight for those that are, you can hide your band's weaknesses. However, the major drawback to this tactic is that artists need to be on-stage to become famous! Backstage workers get the most minimal recognition for their efforts, whereas the frontmen bask in it with each success. Sometimes it's worth diluting the band's appeal in order to ensure that they grow as a unit...

Not only do your artists need to be on stage to exploit their popularity, but they also need to be there to contribute their "Performance" and "Talent" qualities. Mercifully, these are not diluted though. Instead, each member gets to make their contribution and the most successful one determines how the song is received. This may sound like a case for throwing as many people on stage as possible, but quantity is no substitute for quality. 5 average performers may never deliver a 5-star performance amongst them, whereas a solitary superstar always holds that promise. Plus a group effort dilutes the appeal of the band, so the more of them there are the more popular they have to be...

An artist's "role" within a band also determines how their qualities are used. Vocalists, guitarists, and keyboardists are "artistic" roles that effect a 10% increase on the artist's "Talent". Dancers, drummers, and DJ's are "entertaining" roles that offer a 10% increase on "Performance" instead. You can make of this what you will. Your instinct may be to play to a character's strength and make their greatest skill even better, but another school of thought is to hide a character's weaknesses by having them masquerade as something else...    

In the same way that a performer needs to be on-stage to exploit their skills, a producer needs to be backstage to exploit his. By confining an artist to the studio, you can dedicate his skills to production and reap the benefits of a 10% increase. Other than that, the process is similar to that of a performance - where each member contributes to the cause and the most successful one is used. Again, quantity is no substitute for quality as 5-star songs will seldom be produced by average talent. Group work does increase the speed at which songs are produced though - as does delegating backstage roles... 

-------------------------------------------- WHAT MAKES A GOOD SONG? --------------------------------------------

An act is only as good as its material, so a hit-making production team is absolutely essential. When a song is produced, it is blessed with an "entertainment" rating and an "artistic" rating. Entertaining songs are those that have commercial appeal and can be relied upon to sell. Whereas artistic songs are those that warrant critical acclaim and take longer to lose their appeal. Ideally, a song will demonstrate both qualities in abundance - but such gems are extremely rare...

The qualities of your material are inextricably linked to the skills of your production team. The "Production" rating represents an artist's ability to deliver an entertaining song, whilst the "Writing" trait unlocks the magic of an artistic song. Artists with exceptional skill in either category can be relied upon to regularly deliver the goods, whilst those without are lucky if they uncover a single hit. That said, the creative process can be painfully random - so even the finest producers aren't perfect...

When concentrating on an album, you may find that the band's attitude is almost as important as their skills. The "Attitude" rating influences how long the group takes to produce each new track. It could be as soon as next week, or as long as next month! When you're desperate for a hit, that lazy work rate can be hard to tolerate...

-------------------------------- WHAT MAKES A GOOD PERFORMANCE? ----------------------------------------

The other side of the process is bringing the material to life on stage. In the same way that producers unlock the qualities of a song, performers use their skills to deliver it faithfully. The "Performance" rating dictates how likely they are to extract the song's full entertainment potential, whereas the "Talent" rating does the same for a song's artistry...

This is where it becomes painfully clear that the playlist is the backbone of any great act, because even the finest performer is only as good as the material. Once you record a killer track, it's just a question of finding the talent to do it justice. That said, having to perform the material brings its own pressures. In the same way that a top producer can churn out weak songs, even the best of performers can have bad nights. An artist who can consistently perform the material is just as valuable as the producer that brought it to the table...

Consistency is so important because any show worth playing is comprised of multiple songs. A performer needs to deliver every single of them with some degree of expertise in order to satisfy the audience. Just one weak song can sour what would have otherwise been a solid performance. You'll discover as much when the crowd delivers their overall impression at the end of the show. And the point will be drilled home in the subsequent magazine review that analyses your performance! These verdicts are instrumental in your progress, as they dictate whether you're slipping up or making progress. The curious thing is that artists of different calibres have different standards. A 3-star review might benefit a rookie artist, whereas it reflects badly on a veteran who was capable of more...

---------------------------------------- SCHEDULING ISSUES ------------------------------------------

Arranging the performances in the first place is a responsibility in itself. Venues won't request your services until you become a household name, so you spend most of your time trying to arrange concerts through your boss. Since there are dozens of different places to aim for, settling on the right one is a battle of wills similar to the contract negotiations! The boss will suggest an appropriate venue (normally the most easy to arrange), but you may then counter it with where you'd prefer to perform...

For a long time, a lot of doors will be closed to you (especially on the independent circuit, where the boss doesn't have the power to even consider major venues). Your options open up once you become popular enough to sell out the venue in question. In the case of novelty venues such as radio and TV shows, you have to be exceptionally popular to demand to perform there! That luxury is worth chasing though, because televised performances boost your profile and CD sales even more than usual. So much so that many artists accept such bookings regardless of the lower pay-off's...

When it comes to making money on the road, the larger traditional venues are preferable. Each venue has a capacity limit, so it's essential to move up the ladder as your fanbase grows. In addition to choosing where to play, you can also suggest how many songs to perform. Far from a personal preference, this is an important financial decision that dictates how much the tickets cost. Each song adds $5 to the price, so a headline set of 5 songs could bring in as much as $25 per head. Artistically speaking, this is risky business - because the longer a set is, the harder it is to pull off. An exceptionally strong playlist is required to perform more than 3 songs without slipping up...

You need to prove yourself as a live act before you can even suggest such heavyweight sets. Gaining access to a venue is one thing, but getting the clearance for a long set there is quite another. You may find that you can headline a humble hall, whereas you're merely a supporting act at a stadium. So you often have the dilemma of choosing whether to earn a lot of money from a small crowd, or a little money from a large crowd. Becoming popular enough to avoid that compromise is worth fighting for!

Even if you do become a marque act, there's only so many times that you can perform at the same venue. You have to schedule a good mixture of venues so that your drawing power remains intact. This is especially true of novelty venues that aren't designed to accommodate resident acts... 

---------------------------------------- PUBLISHING ISSUES --------------------------------------------

You'll find that your work has a life cycle - ranging from pre-production to post-production. Before you can even consider going on the road, there's a lot of downtime while your new material is recorded. This quiet phase can be filled (and funded) by releasing singles. You can release up to 2 singles from each album, before releasing the album itself...

Record releases can boost your profile as surely as a performance does. In fact, even more so because their qualities are set in stone. It's just that they're few and far between, so they have to be deployed strategically. Record sales are a combination of a song's qualities and your act's popularity. Entertaining songs promise good sales, whereas artistic songs promise a long shelf life. It's important to consider those two factors when releasing a record. Sometimes a humble artistic track can linger around so long that it ends up making more money! 

All records have a shelf life, and will be withdrawn from sale once their appeal has subsided. When you become reasonably successful, this can take a very long time to happen. Fortunately, you don't need to wait until one single has expired before you can release a new one. However, you can only focus on one release at a time - so as soon as you launch a new record, the old one will be discarded. This goes for the album too. Once you release that, all singles lose their market value and must be withdrawn...

Unlike singles, an album's value is determined by the sum of its contents. In the same way that a performance can suffer from a weak delivery, an album can be tainted by too many weak tracks. Recording a truly great album, then, is an incredibly difficult task. Your chances can be improved by going beyond the 8-track minimum. If the original playlist is noticeably feeble, you may be able to salvage it by discovering a handful of bonus tracks. This is a gamble though, because yet another bad track could make things even worse! At best, it could dilute what was otherwise a solid album...

Another reason to pursue a long playlist is that you can charge more for the album when it finally hits the stores. An 8-track album sells for just $10, whereas an epic 14-track album could sell closer to $16. Those few dollars can make all the difference when hundreds of thousands of sales are involved!

When you release a record under a major label, you will usually have to promote it at an "in-store" the following week. This is a golden opportunity to boost sales (as are many other novelty appearances). A strong performance here will ensure that the record gets off to a good start, whereas a bad performance will do more harm than good! One of the worst things about being unsigned is that you don't get these opportunities. Independent releases sell exclusively from word-of-mouth, which can cripple even the finest of records...

---------------------------------------------- RELATIONSHIPS --------------------------------------------

Professional success is one thing, but the music industry is also a political minefield. Almost all artists have allies (whose company they cherish) and rivals (whose company they avoid). Not only does this play out in the press, but it also has an affect on contract negotiations. You will find that enemies refuse to work with each other - and if they already are, they'll cause a lot of friction doing it! On the other hand, friendships can be beneficial to contract negotiations. The promise of working with a friend is almost like a bribe, and will convince any artist to forget any reservations they have. Friends can also be relied upon to keep each other happy, and even help one another progress as artists. For these reasons, it's often worth employing a major artist's friend as a political pawn...

Friendships and rivalries evolve over time - both in the outside world and in your studio. The pressures of working together can cause an internal war between even the greatest of friends. You're not exempt from the heat either. Employees can turn against you if you try their patience - and even complete strangers can take exception to your act! Thankfully, it's just as easy to make friends. You often meet fellow artists backstage at performances and can choose whether or not to socialize with them. Even business meetings can produce friendships if the results are mutually beneficial...

Business transactions can also go sour though. Nowhere are rivalries more venomous than in the courtroom! Whether the claims are bogus or legitimate, it's all too easy to find yourself explaining your actions before a judge. If you're on the wrong side of his verdict, losing a friend will be the least of your concerns...

Perhaps the most important relationship you have is with your boss - whom you have to deal with on a weekly basis. In addition to helping you make progress, he can also crack the whip and make demands of you. Failing to follow his advice, or simply disobeying it, can have disastrous consequences because your career is literally in his hands. He has the power to terminate your contract and demote you to the independent circuit, so it's important to keep him happy. The best way to do that is with a chart-topping, award-winning, money-making act. So get to it, and good luck!
